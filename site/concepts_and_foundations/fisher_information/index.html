Fisher Information¶

Maximum Likelihood Estimation¶

Estimating an unknown parameter \(\varphi\) - here the half-wave plate phase - proceeds by repeating a measurement many times and observing outcomes \(\{i\}\) drawn from a probability distribution \(\{p_i(\varphi)\}\). After \(\nu\) independent trials in which outcome \(i\) occurs \(n_i\) times, the likelihood of the full dataset is multinomial:

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:likelihood} L(\varphi) = \prod_i p_i(\varphi)^{n_i}. \end{equation}\]

The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) \(\hat{\varphi}\) is the value of \(\varphi\) that makes the observed data most probable, i.e. the maximiser of \(L(\varphi)\). In practice one maximises the log-likelihood

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:log_likelihood} \ell(\varphi) = \sum_i n_i \log p_i(\varphi), \end{equation}\]

which is equivalent since the logarithm is monotone, and is numerically more convenient. The MLE is found by solving \(\partial \ell / \partial \varphi = 0\).

Fisher Information from the Log-Likelihood¶

The precision of the MLE is governed by how sharply peaked \(\ell(\varphi)\) is around its maximum: a sharply curved log-likelihood means the data strongly prefers one value of \(\varphi\), while a flat one means many values are nearly equally likely. The curvature is

\[\begin{equation} -\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \varphi^2} = -\sum_i n_i \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2}\log p_i(\varphi). \end{equation}\]

Taking the expectation over many realisations of the experiment, with \(\langle n_i \rangle = \nu\, p_i(\varphi)\), gives

\[\begin{equation} \left\langle -\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \varphi^2} \right\rangle = -\nu \sum_i p_i(\varphi)\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2}\log p_i(\varphi). \end{equation}\]

Expanding the second derivative of the logarithm,

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2}\log p_i(\varphi) = \frac{1}{p_i(\varphi)}\frac{\partial^2 p_i(\varphi)}{\partial\varphi^2} - \frac{1}{p_i(\varphi)^2}\left(\frac{\partial p_i(\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}\right)^2, \end{equation}\]

and substituting back,

\[\begin{equation} \left\langle -\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \varphi^2} \right\rangle = -\nu\sum_i \frac{\partial^2 p_i(\varphi)}{\partial\varphi^2} + \nu\sum_i \frac{1}{p_i(\varphi)}\left(\frac{\partial p_i(\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}\right)^2. \end{equation}\]

The first sum vanishes because the probabilities sum to one for all \(\varphi\),

\[\begin{equation} \sum_i p_i(\varphi) = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_i \frac{\partial^2 p_i(\varphi)}{\partial\varphi^2} = 0, \end{equation}\]

leaving

\[\begin{equation} \left\langle -\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \varphi^2} \right\rangle = \nu \sum_{i} \frac{1}{p_i(\varphi)}\left(\frac{\partial p_i(\varphi)}{\partial \varphi}\right)^2 = \nu\,\mathcal{F}(\varphi). \end{equation}\]

The classical Fisher information is therefore the expected curvature of the log-likelihood per trial:

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:fisher_def} \mathcal{F}(\varphi) = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{p_i(\varphi)}\left(\frac{\partial p_i(\varphi)}{\partial \varphi}\right)^2. \end{equation}\]

A larger Fisher information means the log-likelihood is more sharply peaked around its maximum, so the MLE is more tightly localised and the parameter can be estimated more precisely from the same number of trials.

The Cramér–Rao Bound¶

The connection between \(\mathcal{F}(\varphi)\) and estimation precision is made precise by the Cramér–Rao bound [1][2]: after \(\nu\) independent trials, the variance of any unbiased estimator \(\hat{\varphi}\) satisfies

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:cramer_rao} \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varphi}) \geq \frac{1}{\nu\, \mathcal{F}(\varphi)}. \end{equation}\]

This bound is asymptotically tight: it is achieved by the MLE in the limit of many trials [1], which is the sense in which the MLE is the optimal estimator.

Note that the probabilities \(p_i(\varphi)\) entering equation \(\eqref{eq:fisher_def}\) are conditioned on a click being recorded - they are the outcome probabilities given that at least one detector fired. This conditioning is necessary because trials in which neither detector clicks (\(00\) outcome) are not recorded and carry no phase information. The unnormalized outcome probabilities must therefore be divided by the total click probability \(P_{\mathrm{click}} = 1 - P^{(\eta_H,\eta_V)}(0,0)\) before entering the Fisher information sum. As a consequence, \(\mathcal{F}(\varphi)\) as defined here is the Fisher information per recorded trial, which is the natural figure of merit when comparing to the shot-noise limit defined on the same per-recorded-trial basis.

From Classical to Quantum Fisher Information¶

In a quantum experiment, the choice of measurement is itself a degree of freedom. For a given quantum state \(\hat{\rho}(\varphi)\), different positive-operator valued measures (POVMs) yield different classical Fisher informations. The quantum Fisher information (QFI) \(\mathcal{F}_Q\) is defined as the maximum of \(\mathcal{F}\) over all allowed POVMs [1][2]

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:qfi_def} \mathcal{F}_Q(\varphi) = \max_{\{M_i\}} \mathcal{F}(\varphi), \end{equation}\]

and the resulting quantum Cramér–Rao bound (QCRB)

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:qcrb} \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varphi}) \geq \frac{1}{\nu\, \mathcal{F}_Q(\varphi)} \end{equation}\]

is the fundamental precision limit set by quantum mechanics, independent of which measurement is performed. The QFI depends only on the geometry of the quantum state in parameter space and is saturated by an optimal projective measurement constructed from the symmetric logarithmic derivative of \(\hat{\rho}(\varphi)\) [1].

The measurement considered here is not optimised over all POVMs: it is fixed to a pair of bucket detectors after the PBS, which defines a specific POVM with three outcomes \(\{11, H\text{-only}, V\text{-only}\}\) (see POVM section). The classical Fisher information computed from these three probabilities is therefore a lower bound to the QFI, and quantifies how well this specific measurement scheme can estimate \(\varphi\).

The Shot-Noise Limit¶

The shot-noise limit (SNL), also called the standard quantum limit, is the best precision achievable with \(\bar{n}\) independent (unentangled) probes per recorded trial [2]. For \(N\) independent photons each carrying phase information, the Fisher information scales linearly with the number of probes:

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:snl_general} \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SNL}} = \bar{n}. \end{equation}\]

The corresponding phase uncertainty \(\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{SNL}} = 1/\sqrt{\nu\bar{n}}\) decreases as \(1/\sqrt{\bar{n}}\). Quantum entanglement can in principle push this to the Heisenberg limit \(\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{HL}} = 1/(\sqrt{\nu}\,\bar{n})\), scaling as \(1/\bar{n}\) [2][3]. A key result of quantum metrology is that entanglement is necessary to surpass the shot-noise scaling in a general linear interferometer [2]. Beating the SNL - i.e. achieving \(\mathcal{F}(\varphi) > \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SNL}}\) - is therefore the standard signature of a genuinely quantum metrological advantage.
